In Indian cities, green corridors have often been organised by hand: police officers stationed at each junction along a route, holding traffic as an ambulance carrying an organ or a critical patient passes. It works, but it takes people, planning and time that a routine emergency doesn't have.

A technology-assisted green corridor does the same job with connected signals. Here is the sequence, step by step.

## 1. The route is known

When an emergency vehicle is dispatched, its route is shared with the traffic platform, either from the dispatch system or from the vehicle itself. The platform maps that route onto the junctions it will cross.

## 2. Junctions are reserved ahead of arrival

Each junction on the route is asked to give green to the vehicle's approach shortly before it arrives. The lead time matters: too late and the vehicle meets a queue; too early and the junction holds cross traffic for nothing. Estimating arrival needs the vehicle's position and speed, updated continuously.

## 3. Queues are cleared, cross traffic is held

Turning green early also flushes the vehicles already waiting in front of the ambulance, so they can move out of its way. Cross streets are held on red, with pedestrian phases completed safely before the switch.

## 4. Normal timing returns right behind

Once the vehicle passes, each junction returns to its normal plan, ideally recovering the delay it imposed on other approaches. A corridor that leaves chaos behind it is not a good corridor.

- Safety rules always win: no conflicting greens, no cut-short pedestrian crossings.
- Operators can see and override any corridor from the control room.
- Every activation is logged, so corridors can be reviewed and improved.
