Much of the published work on traffic computer vision comes from roads where vehicles keep to lanes, most of them are cars, and pedestrians use crossings. Point those systems at a busy Indian junction and their assumptions break quickly.

## What makes it hard

- Mixed traffic: two-wheelers, autos, buses, trucks, cycles, carts and pedestrians share the same space.
- Density: vehicles overlap heavily in the frame, so one object hides another.
- Weak lane discipline: trajectories don't follow painted lines, which confuses lane-based logic.
- Conditions: glare, night, rain, dust and haze change how everything looks.
- Long tails: unusual vehicles and situations appear often enough to matter.

## How we approach it

We treat local conditions as the main case. That means training and evaluating on footage that looks like the roads we serve, choosing vehicle classes that match Indian traffic, and tracking objects through occlusion rather than detecting them frame by frame.

It also means reporting performance honestly. A single accuracy number hides more than it shows. We report results separately for day and night, clear and rainy weather, and different camera placements, because a city deserves to know where a system is strong and where it needs help.

> A model is only as good as the hardest street it has been tested on.

## Where people stay in the loop

For enforcement, computer vision produces evidence, not verdicts. A detected violation is an event with images and metadata, queued for review by an authorised officer. That keeps decisions accountable and makes errors visible so they can be corrected.
