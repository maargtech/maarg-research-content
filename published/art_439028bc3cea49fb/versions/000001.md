Most signals in Indian cities run on fixed-time plans. An engineer studies a junction, decides how long each approach should get green, and programs a cycle, sometimes with different plans for morning, evening and night. Once installed, the junction repeats that cycle whether the road in front of it is empty or jammed.

Fixed plans are simple and predictable, and for a junction with steady traffic they work reasonably well. The trouble is that very few junctions have steady traffic. A school lets out, a bus breaks down, rain slows everyone, a cricket match ends. The plan has no way of knowing.

## What 'adaptive' means at one junction

An adaptive junction measures demand as it happens, using cameras or vehicle detectors on each approach, and adjusts its timings within limits that engineers set. If the northbound queue is long and the eastbound road is empty, northbound gets more green this cycle. If a side road has no one waiting, its phase can be shortened or skipped.

- Minimum and maximum green times keep every approach served and pedestrians safe.
- Changes happen cycle by cycle, not second by second, so drivers see consistent behaviour.
- If detection fails, the junction falls back to a safe fixed plan.

## Why corridors matter more than junctions

Optimising one junction in isolation can simply push the queue to the next one. The bigger gain comes from coordination: setting the timing of neighbouring junctions so that a platoon of vehicles released by one arrives at the next as it turns green. This is often called a green wave.

Coordination needs junctions to share a common sense of time and to agree on which direction gets priority along the corridor at a given moment. Adaptive coordination goes one step further and shifts that priority as demand moves through the day.

## What changes in the control room

Adaptive systems produce data as a side effect: queue lengths, flows, delays, detector health. For the first time, operators can see where the network is struggling right now rather than relying on calls and patrols. That visibility is what makes faster incident response, better diversions and useful commuter information possible.

> The signal stops being a timer and becomes a sensor, a decision-maker and a source of truth for the network.

## What adaptive control does not do

It does not create road space. When demand exceeds what a corridor can carry, adaptive control can share delay more fairly and keep queues from spilling into neighbouring junctions, but it cannot make the jam disappear. Honest evaluation compares a corridor before and after under similar conditions and reports the result, whatever it is. That is the standard we hold MAARG Intelligence to.
